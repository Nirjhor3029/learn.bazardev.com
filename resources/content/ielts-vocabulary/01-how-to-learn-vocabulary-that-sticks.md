# Chapter 1 — How to Learn Vocabulary That Actually Sticks

> **Course:** IELTS Vocabulary · **Chapter:** 1 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-vocabulary/01-how-to-learn-vocabulary-that-sticks](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-vocabulary/01-how-to-learn-vocabulary-that-sticks)

If you've ever memorised 50 words from a list the night before and forgotten half of them by morning, the problem isn't your memory — it's the method. This chapter explains **why** random word lists fail, and gives you six concrete techniques this entire course is built around. Every topic chapter after this one (Education, Environment, Technology...) applies these same six methods — so understanding this chapter first makes every later one far more effective.

## 1. Why you forget: the forgetting curve

In 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus ran memory experiments on himself and found that newly learned information fades **fast** — most of it within the first day — unless it's reviewed. But each time you review it, the next drop-off is slower. Review enough times, spaced out, and the information basically stops fading.

This is the entire reason **spaced repetition** (method 5 below) works better than re-reading a list once: you're fighting the natural decay curve on purpose, at the exact points where you're about to forget.

## 2. The six methods, in practice

**Method 1 — Learn in context, not isolation.** A word with no sentence around it is just noise. Every word in this course's topic chapters comes with a real example sentence, because the sentence is what actually gets remembered, not the bare word.

**Method 2 — Learn word families together.** Don't learn "significant" alone — learn its whole family at once, since IELTS examiners specifically reward vocabulary *range* (using different word forms, not repeating the same one).

**Method 3 — Learn collocations, not single words.** Native speakers don't combine words freely — certain words just "go together". Learning the pair is far more useful than learning either word alone:

| Just the word | The collocation (what actually gets used) |
|---|---|
| rise | **a sharp rise** in / **rise sharply** |
| impact | **have a significant impact** on |
| concern | **raise concerns** about |
| issue | **address an issue** |

**Method 4 — Target topics, not random dictionary pages.** IELTS reuses a fairly fixed set of topics (education, environment, technology, health, crime...). Learning 15 words deeply within one topic beats skimming 100 random dictionary entries — this is exactly why chapters 2-12 of this course are organised by topic.

**Method 5 — Use spaced repetition.** Instead of reading a word list once, review it at increasing gaps — roughly day 1, day 3, day 7, day 14 — right when you're about to forget it (see the chart above). A free tool like **Anki** automates this scheduling for you.

**Method 6 — Use it immediately, actively.** Right after learning a word, write your own sentence with it, or say it out loud in a sentence about your own life. Passively re-reading a list is far weaker than producing the word yourself.

## 3. How this course applies all six

Every topic chapter from here on gives you: words grouped into **families** (method 2), each with a real **example sentence** (method 1) and its most common **collocation** (method 3), grouped by **topic** (method 4), plus a quiz you should revisit after a few days, not just once (method 5) — and a prompt to write your own sentence (method 6).

## 4. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. Why does reviewing a word list once usually fail?</strong></summary>

**Because of the forgetting curve** — memory of new information drops sharply within the first day or two without review.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. Instead of memorising "significant" alone, what should you do?</strong></summary>

**Learn its whole word family** — signify (verb), significance (noun), significant (adjective), significantly (adverb) — since IELTS rewards vocabulary range.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Which is more useful to memorise: "rise" alone, or "a sharp rise in"?</strong></summary>

**"A sharp rise in"** — collocations (words that naturally go together) are more usable than isolated words.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. What should you do immediately after learning a new word (method 6)?</strong></summary>

**Write or say your own sentence with it** — active use cements the word far better than passive re-reading.
</details>
