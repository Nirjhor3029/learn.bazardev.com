# Chapter 6 — Future Forms: will, going to, present continuous, future perfect

> **Course:** IELTS Grammar · **Chapter:** 6 of 18
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-grammar/06-future-forms](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-grammar/06-future-forms)

English has no single "future tense" the way it has one past tense form — instead, you pick between several future *forms* depending on **when the decision was made** and **how confident you are**. That distinction (not the time itself) is the real skill in this chapter, and it comes up constantly in IELTS Speaking Part 1 and 3 ("What are your plans for next year?").

Both forms also do a second job — making predictions — and the same decision-timing logic applies there too:

| Form | Job 1 | Job 2 |
|---|---|---|
| **will** | spontaneous decision, made right now | a prediction with **no visible evidence** — a guess/opinion: *"I think it will rain tomorrow."* |
| **going to** | a plan already decided before now | a prediction **based on evidence you can see now**: *"Look at those clouds — it's going to rain."* |

"will" is also used for promises and offers: *"I'll help you with that."*, *"I promise I will call you."*

## 2. Present continuous for fixed arrangements

When a future event is **already arranged** with a specific time and (usually) another person — like a diary entry — English often uses the present continuous instead of "going to":

- *"I'm meeting Sarah at 5pm tomorrow."* (an arrangement — a specific time, with someone else)
- *"I'm going to visit Cox's Bazar next month."* (a personal plan/intention — no fixed appointment)

In everyday speech these two overlap a lot, and IELTS examiners won't penalise you for choosing either — but using the present continuous naturally for a confirmed plan ("I'm flying to Dhaka on Friday") is a small sign of range that sounds more native.

**Form:** will have + past participle. It's used for an action that will be completed **before** a stated future point in time — "by next year," "by the time I'm 30," "by 2030."

## 4. Common mistakes to fix

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|
| I will go to Sarah's party tonight at 8 (already arranged with her). | I'**m going** to Sarah's party tonight at 8. *(prefer continuous for a fixed arrangement)* |
| Look at the sky, I think it will rain. (visible evidence) | Look at the sky, it'**s going to** rain. *(evidence → going to, not will)* |
| By 2030, I will finish my degree. | By 2030, I **will have finished** my degree. *(needs future perfect, not plain "will")* |
| I'll calling you tomorrow. | I**'ll call** you tomorrow. *(after "will," always the base verb)* |

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. The phone rings and you say, without planning it: "___ (I / answer) it."</strong></summary>

**I'll answer** it. — a spontaneous decision made at the moment of speaking → "will."
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. You've already booked your tickets for next week's trip. How do you say it?</strong></summary>

**I'm going to visit / I'm visiting** [place] next week. — the decision was already made before now, so not plain "will."
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. "By the time you read this message, I ___ (leave) the country."</strong></summary>

**will have left** — future perfect: completed before a future reference point ("by the time you read this").
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Someone drops a glass and it's falling. You say: "___"</strong></summary>

**It's going to break!** — a prediction based on evidence you can see right now, not "will."
</details>
