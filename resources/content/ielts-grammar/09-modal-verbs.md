# Chapter 9 — Modal Verbs

> **Course:** IELTS Grammar · **Chapter:** 9 of 18
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-grammar/09-modal-verbs](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-grammar/09-modal-verbs)

Modal verbs (can, could, may, might, must, should, will, would, shall) don't describe an action themselves — they add *meaning* to another verb: ability, possibility, obligation, advice, or a logical guess. One structure rule covers all of them.

## 1. The one structure rule that never changes

The only common exception: **have to** and **ought to** behave like modals in meaning but keep "to", and **have to** conjugates like a normal verb (*has to*, *had to*).

## 2. Ability: can, could, be able to

| Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| can | ability right now / general | *I can swim.* |
| could | general ability in the past | *When I was young, I could run fast.* |
| was/were able to | **one specific** past success (not "could") | *I was able to finish the exam early.* |
| will be able to | future ability | *After this course, you will be able to write a Band 7 essay.* |

**The trap:** use "could" for a general past ability, but **"was/were able to"** (not "could") for one specific achievement — *"I could pass the exam yesterday"* is wrong; *"I was able to pass the exam yesterday"* is correct. ("Couldn't" has no such restriction — it works for both.)

## 3. Possibility: may, might, could

All three mean "it's possible" with roughly the same strength — **may** is slightly more formal/likely, **might** slightly more uncertain, in practice they're often interchangeable:

- *It may rain later.* / *It might rain later.* / *It could rain later.*
- *She may/might/could be at home.*

## 4. Obligation and necessity: must vs. have to, mustn't vs. don't have to

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must | obligation from **the speaker's own** feeling/authority | *I must call my mother tonight.* |
| have to | obligation from an **external** rule or situation | *You have to wear a uniform at this school.* |
| mustn't | **prohibited** — not allowed | *You mustn't park here.* |
| don't have to | **not necessary** — free choice, no prohibition | *You don't have to attend; it's optional.* |

**The classic IELTS trap:** "mustn't" and "don't have to" look similar but mean opposite things. *"You mustn't come"* = you are forbidden. *"You don't have to come"* = coming is optional, not forbidden.

## 5. Advice: should, ought to, had better

| Form | Strength | Example |
|---|---|---|
| should / ought to | general advice or recommendation | *You should see a doctor.* |
| had better | stronger — implies a bad consequence if ignored | *You'd better hurry, or you'll miss the bus.* |

## 6. Deduction: making a logical guess from evidence

This is the most tested modal use in IELTS Speaking/Listening. You're not stating a fact — you're guessing how likely it is, based on evidence.

To guess about the **past**, use **modal + have + past participle**:

- *He must have left already.* (I'm almost sure he left.)
- *She might have forgotten.* (maybe she forgot.)
- *They can't have arrived yet.* (I'm almost sure they haven't arrived — it's too early.)

**Common error:** writing *"must has left"* or *"must left"* — it's always **must have + past participle**, never "must has" or "must + past tense".

## 7. Common mistakes to fix

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|
| She can to swim. | She **can swim**. *(no "to" after a modal)* |
| I could pass the exam yesterday. | I **was able to** pass the exam yesterday. *(one specific past success)* |
| You mustn't come if you're busy. *(meaning "it's optional")* | You **don't have to** come if you're busy. |
| He must has left already. | He **must have** left already. |
| You should to see a doctor. | You **should** see a doctor. |

## 8. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. "I ___ finish the whole exam in time yesterday — it was tight, but I managed it." (could / was able to)</strong></summary>

**was able to** — a single specific past success, not general ability.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. "You ___ park here — it's a fire lane." (mustn't / don't have to)</strong></summary>

**mustn't** — this is a prohibition, not an optional choice.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. "The lights are off and the car is gone — they ___ be home." (must / can't)</strong></summary>

**can't** — strong evidence they are NOT home.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Fix: "She must has forgotten her keys."</strong></summary>

**She must have forgotten her keys.** — always "must have + past participle."
</details>
