# Chapter 4 — Extending Your Cue-Card Answer

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 4 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/04-extending-your-cue-card-answer](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/04-extending-your-cue-card-answer)

Chapter 3 covered the shape of a cue card and how to use the 1-minute prep. This chapter deals with the real-world problem candidates actually run into: **the topic you're handed isn't one you have a ready story for.** "Describe a law you think should change" or "Describe a piece of technology you find useful" — what if nothing comes to mind in that first second?

## 1. The "borrow and bend" technique

You are never required to tell the literal truth — examiners score your **language**, not the accuracy of your biography. If the real topic is unfamiliar, **borrow** the closest real experience you have and **bend** it to fit the card.

This isn't "lying" in a way that matters — it's the same technique essay writers use when they don't have a perfect real example. What matters to the examiner is fluent, coherent, well-structured language, not biographical accuracy.

## 2. If your mind still goes blank: the 5 W's fallback

When even "borrow and bend" doesn't produce anything in the first few seconds, fall back on the 5 W's as a structure, answering each one in turn about *any* related thing you can think of:

- **What** is it?
- **Where/when** did you first encounter it?
- **Who** else was involved?
- **Why** does it matter (to you, or in general)?
- **How** did you feel about it, or what happened next?

Answering these five in order almost always produces close to 2 minutes of natural speech, regardless of topic.

## 3. Staying on topic while still extending

A common fear is "if I add detail, I'll go off-topic." In practice, the opposite risk is more common — answers that are too short. Safe ways to add length *without* drifting:

- A **specific example** ("for instance, last year…")
- A **comparison** ("unlike X, this…")
- A brief **cause or consequence** ("because of this, I…")
- A short **personal reaction** ("I found that quite surprising, because…")

Each of these naturally ties back to the same topic, unlike adding an unrelated second story.

## 4. A worked example

> **Card:** Describe a piece of technology you find useful.
> **Notes:** *smartphone — translation app — learning English — 1 year — saves time*

> **Spoken answer (roughly 90 seconds):** "I'd like to talk about my smartphone, specifically a translation app I use almost every day. I started using it about a year ago, when I was trying to read English news articles for my studies. Whenever I come across a word I don't know, I just point my camera at it, and the app translates it instantly — it's much faster than looking words up in a dictionary one by one. What I find most useful is that it also saves the words I've looked up, so I can review them later, almost like a personal vocabulary list. Honestly, it's changed how I study — I read far more in English now than I used to, simply because looking up a word no longer breaks my concentration."

Notice: one clear topic, a timeline, a specific detail (the camera feature), and a short reflection at the end — the same structure from Chapter 3, filled with real, specific content.

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. What does "borrow and bend" mean?</strong></summary>

Taking the **closest real experience** you actually have and adapting it to fit an unfamiliar cue-card topic, since examiners score language, not biographical accuracy.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. What are the 5 W's fallback questions from this chapter?</strong></summary>

**What, Where/when, Who, Why, How** — answering each in turn about any related thing produces close to 2 minutes of speech.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Name two safe ways to add length to an answer without going off-topic.</strong></summary>

Any two of: **a specific example, a comparison, a cause/consequence, a short personal reaction.**
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Is it acceptable to adapt a real memory to fit a cue card, rather than telling the literal truth?</strong></summary>

**Yes** — examiners score the language you produce, not the factual accuracy of your story.
</details>
