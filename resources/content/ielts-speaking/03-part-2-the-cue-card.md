# Chapter 3 — Part 2: The Cue Card

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 3 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/03-part-2-the-cue-card](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/03-part-2-the-cue-card)

Part 2 is the part most candidates dread: the examiner hands you a card with a topic and 3-4 bullet points, gives you **1 minute** to prepare with a pencil and paper, then expects you to talk **alone, without interruption, for up to 2 minutes**. No conversation to lean on — just you.

## 1. What a cue card actually looks like

A typical card:

> **Describe a skill you learned that you think is useful.**
> You should say:
> - what the skill is
> - how you learned it
> - how long it took you to learn
> - and explain why you think this skill is useful

Every cue card has this same shape: **one topic + 3-4 bullet points + one "and explain…" point**. The bullets are prompts to structure your talk, not a checklist you must tick off word-for-word.

The examiner will not interrupt you during your 2 minutes, even if you finish early or run out of things to say — this is why planning in the 1-minute window matters so much.

## 3. Using the 1 minute well: the 4-box note method

Don't write full sentences — you won't have time, and you don't want to read from a script (examiners notice, and it hurts Fluency). Instead, write **one keyword per bullet point**, in four quick boxes:

| Bullet (from the card) | Your note (keywords only) |
|---|---|
| What the skill is | *cooking — Bengali curry* |
| How you learned it | *YouTube, 2020, lockdown* |
| How long it took | *~3 months, weekends* |
| Why it's useful | *saves money, cook for family* |

Four or five words per box is plenty — they're memory triggers while you talk, not a script to read aloud.

## 4. A simple structure to fill the 2 minutes

1. **Introduce the topic in one sentence** ("I'd like to talk about a skill I learned a few years ago — cooking.")
2. **Work through your notes in order**, expanding each keyword into 2-3 full sentences.
3. **Add one small story or example** — a specific moment makes the talk feel real, not generic.
4. **Close with a short reflection** on why it matters to you now.

If you finish before 2 minutes, that's fine — the examiner moves on. **Don't panic-fill with repetition**; it's better to stop a little early with clear content than to pad with filler.

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. How much time do you get to prepare before speaking in Part 2?</strong></summary>

**1 minute**, with pencil and paper to make notes.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. How long can you speak for in Part 2?</strong></summary>

**Up to 2 minutes**, without the examiner interrupting.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Should you write full sentences during the 1-minute prep time?</strong></summary>

**No** — write short **keywords**, one per bullet point, as memory triggers rather than a script.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. What should you do if you finish speaking before the 2 minutes are up?</strong></summary>

**Stop naturally** — don't panic-fill with repetition; the examiner will move on to the follow-up questions.
</details>
