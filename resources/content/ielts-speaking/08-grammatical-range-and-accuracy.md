# Chapter 8 — Grammatical Range and Accuracy

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 8 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/08-grammatical-range-and-accuracy](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/08-grammatical-range-and-accuracy)

This criterion has a trap built into its name: candidates hear "accuracy" and focus only on avoiding mistakes, forgetting the word right before it — **range**. A perfectly correct answer made entirely of short, simple sentences cannot reach the top bands. You need both variety *and* correctness.

The IELTS band descriptors are explicit about this: a candidate using only simple structures, even flawlessly, is capped well below one using a genuine mix, even with occasional slips.

## 2. What "complex sentence structures" actually means in speech

You don't need academic grammar — just natural ways of connecting ideas that go beyond "Subject + Verb + Object":

| Structure type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Relative clause** | "The job that I have now is quite different from my last one." |
| **Conditional** | "If I had more free time, I'd probably learn another language." |
| **Comparative** | "Living in a city is more convenient, but it's also more expensive than a village." |
| **Time clause** | "Before I started this job, I used to work night shifts." |

Using even two or three of these naturally across your 11-14 minutes shows range — you don't need every sentence to be complex.

## 3. A realistic view of "accuracy"

Examiners do **not** expect error-free speech — even fluent native speakers make small grammar slips when talking spontaneously. What's actually scored:

- **Frequency of errors** — occasional slips are expected; frequent, basic errors (like consistent subject-verb disagreement) cap your score.
- **Whether an error blocks communication** — a tense slip that doesn't confuse the listener is far less costly than one that makes your meaning unclear.
- **Self-correction** — correcting yourself when you notice a slip is seen positively, not negatively (Chapter 6 covers this too).

## 4. Common Bangladeshi-English grammar patterns worth watching

A few patterns that show up often and are easy to self-check:

- **Tense consistency**: switching between past and present mid-story without reason ("I went to the market and I am buying vegetables" → should stay in one tense: "I went... and bought...").
- **Articles**: "a/an/the" are often dropped or misused ("I am going to market" → "I am going to **the** market").
- **Subject-verb agreement with "there is/are"**: "There is many reasons" → "There **are** many reasons."

None of these need to be perfect every time — but noticing them in your own practice recordings (see Chapter 12's mock test) helps reduce how often they appear.

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. Can an error-free answer made only of short, simple sentences reach the top Speaking bands?</strong></summary>

**No** — the band descriptors cap scores when range is missing, even with zero grammar errors.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. Name two types of "complex" sentence structures useful in spoken English.</strong></summary>

Any two of: **relative clause, conditional, comparative, time clause.**
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Do examiners expect completely error-free speech?</strong></summary>

**No** — occasional slips are expected and normal; what matters is frequency and whether an error blocks communication.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Is correcting yourself mid-sentence when you notice a grammar slip seen positively or negatively?</strong></summary>

**Positively** — self-correction shows awareness of your own language, which examiners view well.
</details>
