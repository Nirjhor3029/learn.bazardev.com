# Chapter 5 — Part 3: Discussion Questions

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 5 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/05-part-3-discussion-questions](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/05-part-3-discussion-questions)

Part 3 is a 4-5 minute discussion built directly on your Part 2 topic — but it zooms out from your personal experience into **general, abstract, and opinion-based** questions. If Part 2 asked "describe a skill you learned," Part 3 asks "why do you think some skills are harder to learn as an adult?" This is where the highest bands are usually earned or lost.

The shift matters: in Part 3, "I" matters less than "people in general" or "society." Answering a Part 3 question with only your own personal story, without generalising, is a common way candidates underperform here.

## 2. From personal to general: a quick conversion

| Part 2-style (personal) | Part 3-style (general) |
|---|---|
| "I learned to cook using YouTube videos." | "Many people today learn practical skills through online videos rather than formal classes, because it's flexible and free." |
| "I think my hometown has gotten more crowded." | "Urbanisation is a trend affecting most mid-sized cities, often because of better job opportunities in city centres." |

Notice the general version uses **people/society-level language** ("many people," "a trend," "often because") instead of first-person narrative.

## 3. Giving an opinion, and defending it briefly

Part 3 regularly asks "Do you agree that…?" or "Why do you think…?" A strong answer has three parts:

1. **State your position clearly** — don't hedge with "maybe, I don't know."
2. **Give one clear reason.**
3. **Add a brief example or counterpoint** to show you've considered more than one side.

> "I'd say I partly agree. Online learning does make skills more accessible, since anyone with internet access can start learning for free. That said, some skills, like a musical instrument, probably still benefit from in-person feedback that a video can't give."

This shows reasoning, not just opinion — which is exactly what Grammatical Range (Chapter 8) and coherence (Chapter 6) are scored on.

## 4. When you don't have a strong opinion

It's fine to say you're genuinely unsure — examiners don't need you to have a fixed position. What matters is that you **reason out loud**:

> "That's a difficult question, actually. On one hand, you could argue X, because... But on the other hand, Y also seems true, since..."

This "thinking out loud" style is natural, coherent, and still gives the examiner plenty to score — far better than freezing or giving a flat, unsupported answer.

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. How does Part 3 differ from Part 2 in terms of what questions ask about?</strong></summary>

Part 3 questions are **general and abstract** (about people/society), while Part 2 is about **one specific personal example**.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. What are the three parts of a strong opinion answer in Part 3?</strong></summary>

**State a clear position → give one reason → add a brief example or counterpoint.**
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Is it acceptable to say you're unsure about a Part 3 question?</strong></summary>

**Yes** — as long as you reason out loud by weighing both sides, rather than freezing or giving a flat, unsupported answer.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. What phrase-level change turns a personal sentence into a general one?</strong></summary>

Switching from first-person narrative ("I learned...") to **people/society-level language** ("many people...", "a trend...").
</details>
