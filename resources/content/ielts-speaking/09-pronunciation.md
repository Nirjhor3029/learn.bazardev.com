# Chapter 9 — Pronunciation

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 9 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/09-pronunciation](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/09-pronunciation)

Pronunciation is the last of the four 25%-weighted criteria, and the most misunderstood. As Chapter 1 established, **your accent is not scored** — what's scored is whether the examiner can understand you with reasonable ease, and whether you use features like stress and intonation to make meaning clear.

Accent is "how" you produce sounds; these four features are "whether the message comes across clearly" — that's the real distinction examiners are trained to make.

## 2. Word stress: the syllable that changes meaning

English word stress isn't random, and getting it wrong can genuinely confuse a listener, since some words change meaning depending on which syllable is stressed:

| Word | Stress on first syllable | Stress on second syllable |
|---|---|---|
| **record** | **RE**-cord (noun: a record) | re-**CORD** (verb: to record) |
| **present** | **PRE**-sent (noun: a gift) | pre-**SENT** (verb: to present) |

For most everyday vocabulary, consistently stressing the wrong syllable (common patterns from Bangla-influenced English include over-stressing the final syllable) can make otherwise correct words harder to recognise.

## 3. Sentence stress: emphasise the words that carry meaning

Not every word in a sentence gets equal emphasis. Content words (nouns, verbs, key adjectives) are usually stressed; small grammar words (a, the, is, to) usually aren't:

> "I **REALLY** enjoyed the **TRIP**, but the **WEATHER** was quite **DISAPPOINTING**."

Stressing the right words (here: *really*, *trip*, *weather*, *disappointing*) is what makes speech sound natural rather than flat and monotone — and it's a skill, not a talent, that improves with deliberate practice.

## 4. Chunking: speaking in phrases, not word-by-word

Reading each word with an equal, robotic pause between them is a common habit that hurts clarity, even when every word is pronounced correctly. Natural English groups words into small "chunks" with a brief pause between chunks, not between every word:

> Robotic: "I — went — to — the — market — yesterday — to — buy — some — vegetables."
> Natural chunking: "I went to the market yesterday / to buy some vegetables."

Practising reading a sentence aloud and marking natural pause points with a "/" is a simple way to build this habit before the test.

## 5. A simple daily practice routine

1. **Record yourself** answering a Part 1 or Part 2 question for 1-2 minutes (your phone's voice recorder is enough).
2. **Listen back once without judging** — just notice overall clarity.
3. **Listen a second time**, marking any word you mispronounced or any place you spoke word-by-word instead of in chunks.
4. **Re-record the same answer**, deliberately fixing just those 1-2 things.

Five minutes of this, a few times a week, improves pronunciation far more than reading pronunciation rules silently.

## 6. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. Is your accent scored under Pronunciation?</strong></summary>

**No** — clarity and features like stress and intonation are scored, not the accent itself.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. What's the difference in meaning between RE-cord and re-CORD?</strong></summary>

**RE-cord** (stress on first syllable) is the noun ("a record"); **re-CORD** (stress on second syllable) is the verb ("to record").
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. In the sentence "I really enjoyed the trip," which type of word usually gets sentence stress — content words or small grammar words?</strong></summary>

**Content words** (nouns, verbs, key adjectives) — not small grammar words like "the" or "a."
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. What is "chunking" in speech?</strong></summary>

Grouping words into **natural phrases** with brief pauses between phrases, instead of pausing equally between every single word.
</details>
