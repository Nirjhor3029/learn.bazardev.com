# Chapter 6 — Fluency and Coherence

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 6 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/06-fluency-and-coherence](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/06-fluency-and-coherence)

Fluency and Coherence is the first of the four 25%-weighted criteria. It's often misunderstood as "speaking fast." It isn't. **Fluency** is about speaking at a natural pace without long, unnatural pauses or constant self-restarting. **Coherence** is about your ideas connecting logically, so a listener can follow your reasoning without effort.

A candidate who speaks quickly but jumps between unrelated ideas scores lower on Coherence even though they sound "fluent" in the everyday sense. A candidate who speaks at a relaxed, steady pace with clear logical order can score well on both.

## 2. Fillers: useful in moderation, harmful in excess

| Filler type | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Word-based filler** (used sparingly) | "well," "actually," "you know" | Can buy a second of thinking time naturally |
| **Sound-based filler** (overused) | "umm... umm... ahh..." repeated constantly | Breaks fluency and sounds hesitant |
| **Silent filler** (long pause) | 4-5 seconds of silence before answering | Strongly hurts Fluency |

A little hesitation is completely normal, even for native speakers — the problem is **frequent long pauses or constant "umm"** that interrupt the flow of ideas.

## 3. Linking words that actually help Coherence

Good linking isn't about stuffing in advanced words — it's about using the *right* connector so your logic is clear:

- **Adding an idea:** "also," "in addition," "on top of that"
- **Contrasting:** "however," "on the other hand," "that said"
- **Giving a reason:** "because," "since," "the reason is"
- **Giving a result:** "so," "as a result," "which means"
- **Sequencing:** "first," "after that," "eventually"

> Weak: "I like my job. It's hard. I'm tired. I like the people."
> Better: "I like my job, even though it's quite demanding — I'm often tired by the end of the day, but I stick with it mainly because of the people I work with."

The second version isn't longer vocabulary — it's the **same ideas, connected logically**.

## 4. Self-correction: fine, if brief

If you realise mid-sentence that you made a mistake, a quick self-correction ("I mean..." or "sorry, I meant...") is completely normal and not penalised — examiners don't expect 100% accuracy in live speech. What hurts Fluency is **repeated, lengthy** self-correction that derails the whole sentence.

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. What's the difference between Fluency and Coherence?</strong></summary>

**Fluency** is pace and flow (not speed) with few long pauses; **Coherence** is whether your ideas connect logically for the listener.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. Is occasional "well," "actually," or "you know" harmful to your Fluency score?</strong></summary>

**No** — used sparingly, these are normal thinking-time fillers; the problem is **frequent, long** pauses or repeated "umm."
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. Give one linking word each for adding an idea and giving a reason.</strong></summary>

Any valid pair, e.g. **"also"** (adding) and **"because"** (reason).
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Is a brief self-correction mid-sentence penalised?</strong></summary>

**No** — a quick, natural self-correction is normal; only repeated, lengthy self-correction hurts Fluency.
</details>
