# Chapter 10 — Preparing Common Topics Without Memorising a Script

> **Course:** IELTS Speaking · **Chapter:** 10 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/10-preparing-topics-without-memorising](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-speaking/10-preparing-topics-without-memorising)

It's natural to want to prepare answers in advance for common topics — hometown, family, hobbies, studies, technology. The trap is turning that preparation into a **memorised script**, which examiners are specifically trained to detect, and which actively hurts your score rather than helping it.

Examiners are trained specifically to recognise memorised or templated answers, and the official guidance is explicit: this results in a lower score for Fluency and Coherence, since memorised speech isn't genuinely spontaneous — it's reading from memory.

## 2. What to prepare instead: ideas, not sentences

The fix isn't "don't prepare" — it's preparing the **right thing**. Instead of memorising full sentences, prepare:

- **3-4 keyword ideas** per common topic (not full answers)
- **Useful vocabulary** related to the topic (not fixed phrases to recite)
- **A flexible structure** you can apply to any question on that theme (the extension formula from Chapter 2, or the note-box method from Chapter 3)

| Instead of memorising... | Prepare this instead |
|---|---|
| "My hometown is a beautiful place with rich culture and friendly people..." (full script) | Keywords: *quiet, small, friendly neighbours, near a river* — build the sentence live, each time |
| A fixed answer for "describe your best friend" | Keywords about a real friend: *known since school, honest, helps me study* |

This way, whatever the exact wording of the question, you have real content ready — just not a fixed script to recite.

## 3. Common topic categories worth preparing ideas for

Rather than guessing exact questions, prepare flexible ideas across a handful of broad categories that cover most Part 1 and Part 2 topics:

- **People** (family member, friend, someone you admire)
- **Places** (hometown, a place you've visited, your home)
- **Things** (an object you own, a gift, technology you use)
- **Events** (a celebration, a memorable day, an achievement)
- **Activities** (a hobby, a skill, how you spend free time)

Most real cue cards fall into one of these five buckets — having 2-3 real memories ready per bucket covers a huge share of possible topics.

## 4. A practical way to "practice" without memorising

1. Pick one category above.
2. Give yourself exactly 1 minute to jot keywords (as in Chapter 3).
3. Speak for up to 2 minutes, out loud, timed.
4. The next day, pick a **different but related** question from the same category, and speak again — using the same real memory, but phrased freshly.

Doing this repeatedly builds genuine fluency with your own real content, instead of a script that collapses the moment the real question differs even slightly from what you rehearsed.

<summary><strong>Q2. What should you prepare instead of full memorised sentences?</strong></summary>

**Keyword ideas, relevant vocabulary, and a flexible structure** — built fresh each time, not a fixed script.

Any three of: **People, Places, Things, Events, Activities.**
