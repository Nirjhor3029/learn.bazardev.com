# Chapter 2 — Form and Note Completion

> **Course:** IELTS Listening · **Chapter:** 2 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-listening/02-form-and-note-completion](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-listening/02-form-and-note-completion)

Form completion is the most common Section 1 question type — usually a booking, registration, or enquiry call. Note completion is the same skill applied to Section 2-4's more academic content. Both reward the same preparation: turning every gap into a question **before** the audio starts.

Converting "Surname: ___" into "What is the surname?" forces you to listen actively for a specific answer, rather than passively waiting for words to match.

## 2. What usually fills the gap

| Field type | What to expect | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Names** | Often spelled out letter by letter | Easily confused letters: B/P/D, M/N, S/F |
| **Numbers** (phone, reference, price) | Read digit by digit, or with "double" (e.g., "double 5" = 55) | A pause or correction mid-number |
| **Dates** | Day + month, sometimes said in different orders | "the fourteenth of March" vs "March the fourteenth" |
| **Addresses** | Combination of a number, street name, and sometimes a spelled word | Street names are often spelled if unusual |

## 3. Forms and notes follow the conversation's order

Just like Reading's completion types, the gaps almost always follow the order of the conversation — gap 1 comes before gap 2 in what you hear. If you miss one, don't panic and go back; move on and listen for the next gap's cue instead.

## 4. The word-limit rule still applies

The instructions will say something like "Write **ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER** for each answer." This is the exact same rule from Reading's completion chapters — exceeding it scores zero, however correct the content.

## 5. Common mistakes to fix

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|
| Waiting passively for the gap's exact answer to appear. | Turn each gap into a question before listening, so you're actively hunting for it. |
| Freezing when you miss one gap, and losing the next few. | Move on immediately — listen for the next gap's cue rather than dwelling on a miss. |
| Writing only part of a spelled-out name, guessing the rest. | Listen for every letter; confusing letters like B/P or M/N are common distractors. |
| Ignoring the word limit when the answer "feels" longer. | Respect the stated limit exactly, even if a longer phrase would feel more natural. |

## 6. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. Before the audio starts, what should you do with a gap like "Surname: ___"?</strong></summary>

**Turn it into a question** — "What is the surname?" — so you're actively listening for a specific answer.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. You miss the answer to gap 3. What should you do?</strong></summary>

**Move on immediately** to gap 4's cue — don't dwell on the miss, since gaps follow the conversation's order.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. A name is spelled out as "B... P... D...". What's the main risk here?</strong></summary>

**Confusing similar-sounding letters** like B, P, and D — listen carefully to each one.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. The instructions say "ONE WORD AND/OR A NUMBER." Your correct answer is two words. What happens?</strong></summary>

**It scores zero** — exceeding the word limit loses the mark regardless of correctness.
</details>
