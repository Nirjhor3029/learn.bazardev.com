British English is the most common and is always used for single-speaker sections (like Section 4). Get regular exposure to all four — BBC and CNN are good free sources — rather than only practising with one accent.

## 2. Signposting language: order and relation

| Function | Example phrases | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Order** | "firstly," "to begin with," "next," "finally" | Where you are in the speaker's structure — useful for note completion in Section 4 |
| **Relation** | "however," "in addition," "as a result," "on the other hand" | How the current idea connects to the last one — often marks a shift relevant to the answer |

These phrases exist specifically to help listeners follow structure — tune your ear to them the way you'd watch for sequencing words in a flow-chart.

## 3. Keeping pace across four increasingly difficult sections

The audio runs continuously — there's no way to pause and re-read like in Reading. Two habits help:

1. **Use every short pause between sections** to read the next section's questions and predict word types — don't spend it reviewing what you just finished.
2. **If you miss a question, let it go immediately.** Dwelling on a miss in Section 3 costs you the next few answers; the audio doesn't wait.

## 4. Common mistakes to fix

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|
| Only practising with one accent (usually British). | Get regular exposure to all four accent families used in the test. |
| Ignoring signpost phrases like "however" or "firstly." | Tune in to them — they mark structure and often sit near an answer. |
| Spending the between-section pause reviewing past answers. | Use it to read ahead and predict the next section's answers instead. |
| Freezing after a missed question and losing the next ones too. | Let a miss go immediately — the audio keeps moving regardless. |
