# Chapter 2 — True/False/Not Given and Yes/No/Not Given Questions

> **Course:** IELTS Reading · **Chapter:** 2 of 12
> Read on the website: [learn.bazardev.com/ielts-reading/02-true-false-not-given](https://learn.bazardev.com/ielts-reading/02-true-false-not-given)

This question type tests the paraphrase skill from Chapter 1 most directly — and causes more lost marks than any other type, almost always for the same reason: confusing **False** with **Not Given**.

## 1. Two formats, one logic

| Format | Used for | "Opposite of true" is called |
|---|---|---|
| **True / False / Not Given** | factual information in the passage | False |
| **Yes / No / Not Given** | the writer's opinions or claims | No |

Always check the instructions before you start — the underlying logic is identical either way.

**The test that separates False from Not Given:** does the passage actually **contradict** the statement, or does it simply **never mention** the specific claim? If it's silent on the claim, the answer is Not Given — not False.

## 3. The #1 trap: qualifier words

Examiners often insert a single word that shifts a statement's scope. A small word like "all," "some," "always," or "mainly" can completely change whether a statement matches the passage.

Because the passage directly limits the claim to "some," a statement claiming "all" is **contradicted**, not just unaddressed — so the correct answer is **False**, not Not Given.

## 4. Other common mistakes to fix

| ❌ Wrong | ✅ Correct |
|---|---|
| Marking "False" because a fact simply isn't mentioned in the passage. | If the passage is silent on the claim, the answer is **Not Given**. |
| Marking "True" because the statement shares vocabulary with the passage. | Shared words don't guarantee a match — compare the **meaning**, since paraphrase can reuse words with a different sense. |
| Marking "True" using outside/real-world knowledge the passage doesn't state. | Answer based **only** on what the passage actually says. |
| Ignoring small qualifier words like "mainly," "only," or "occasionally." | Underline qualifier words in every statement — they frequently decide the answer. |

## 5. Quick practice

<details>
<summary><strong>Q1. The passage never mentions a fact that happens to be historically true. What's the answer?</strong></summary>

**Not Given** — the passage's content decides the answer, not outside knowledge.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q2. The passage says "some scientists believe X." The statement says "all scientists believe X." What's the answer?</strong></summary>

**False** — "some" directly contradicts "all"; this isn't a case of the passage simply being silent.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q3. A statement shares several exact words with the passage. Does that guarantee the answer is True?</strong></summary>

**No** — shared vocabulary doesn't guarantee a matching meaning; compare what's actually being claimed.
</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Q4. Which question format is used for the writer's opinions rather than plain facts?</strong></summary>

**Yes/No/Not Given.**
</details>
